The rapid spread of false or misleading information on social media platforms is undermining the progress of digital development. Disinformation is designed to manipulate facts, is often spread by undemocratic actors to advance their agendas and mislead the public and can sow confusion around major events such as elections, conflicts, and epidemics. Government actors can also use disinformation to suppress opposition, threatening democratic processes. 
 
Social media platforms have been especially weaponized to spread disinformation. There is a higher probability of false information going viral because algorithms are designed to maximize engagement on content that is controversial and more likely to generate likes and shares, causing disinformation to spread at an alarming rate.
 
Digital development projects can mitigate the effects of disinformation by designing their programs with these risks in mind. This blog will explore three steps organizations can take to mitigate the negative effects of disinformation and build information resilience. 

## Increase the Capacity of Fact-Checking Organizations

Global fact-checking efforts must constantly evolve to stay up to date with new types of content, from the spread of AI-generated media to the emergence of realistic deepfakes. Donors and development agencies can support media accountability and integrity by increasing funding towards building the capacity of journalists and fact-checkers working in this space. 

[FactSpace](https://factspace.org/) West Africa is one such organization. As part of a pilot program with five grantees funded by the Conflict, Peacebuilding, and Governance Division in the USAID Africa Bureau, FactSpace is expanding its fact-checking capabilities in Ghana, Liberia, Nigeria, Sierra Leone, and the Gambia. Organizations like FactSpace play a critical role in checking claims made by influential figures and providing independent, non-partisan reporting to curb disinformation. However, fact-checkers are fighting an uphill battle in the age of social media and amid a dip in support for fact-checking from social media companies themselves. Through the grant program, FactSpace, with training facilitated by fellow grantee Centre for Analytics and Behavioural Change (CABC), is getting access to Brandwatch, a social media analytics tool that would be out of budget for many non-profit organizations. Brandwatch has become a key tool for FactSpace in their research, as demonstrated by their recent article analyzing the role of social media activism in a protest in Ghana.

But what is social media analytics and why is it an important tool for fact-checkers and development organizations in countering disinformation?

## Leverage Social Media Analytics

Social media analytics (SMA) is a process used by fact-checkers, organizations, and businesses to understand trends on social media platforms and analyze user behavior. In this context, using SMA tools will not explicitly differentiate between true and false claims, but it can still be a way to observe data, recognize patterns, and track shifts in opinions, all of which help to detect instances of disinformation. SMA tools like Brandwatch and Meltwater can track keywords and the contexts they appear in. They can find key players that are involved in spreading certain false claims, locations where suspicious activity is most common, and users who frequently face hate speech online. USAID grantees Pollicy and Nendo, for example, are using SMA to examine instances of technology-facilitated gender-based violence (TFGBV) in Kenya. Pollicy and Nendo will then use these findings to facilitate a co-creation workshop to discuss solutions and recommendations to address TFGBV.

There are diverse use-cases for SMA, such as helping development practitioners understand the scale of disinformation, hate speech, or TFGBV in areas where they plan to implement programming. Development practitioners can use the data to design their programs with mitigation strategies in mind. The Conflict, Peacebuilding and Governance Division in the USAID Africa Bureau commissioned a how-to guide for USAID Missions or implementing partners who may want to incorporate SMA into their activity design. Findings from SMA may be used for programming such as behavioral change campaigns to raise awareness about disinformation and change the public’s attitudes and actions through targeted interventions.

## Design Media Literacy and Digital Safety Initiatives 

In addition to incorporating SMA into their activities, development practitioners, donors, and grant seekers may want to consider incorporating media literacy initiatives into future programming. These initiatives can train social media users to evaluate, analyze, and be skeptical of the validity of the information they consume. Media literacy is a key factor when designing programs to counter digital risks and threats and there are numerous resources that projects can draw from, such as IREX’s Learn To Discern curriculum, OECD’s Digital Media Literacy Tips, or ISD’s 101 of Disinformation Detection. These resources include topics like learning to investigate the source, checking the evidence, and watching for unusual formatting to discern disinformation.

Considering the rapid growth of the digital ecosystem in many places, it is also important to incorporate digital safety awareness and training in development programming. USAID’s Digital Asia Accelerator, a project under the Digital Connectivity and Cybersecurity Partnership initiative, launched the Wai Kid Digital University Challenge in Thailand. The initiative challenged university students to create and disseminate informational videos for the public to practice digital safety by learning how to spot fake information, keep their personal information safe online, and protect themselves from cyberbullying. Through this challenge, the participants themselves became more aware of the topic while championing digital safety and helping educate the public in a creative way. 

## Conclusion

Countering digital threats and the spread of disinformation has become a top priority for digital development projects. And while the landscape is constantly evolving and the tactics to spread disinformation are becoming more manipulative and sophisticated, so are the tools and the approaches designed to combat it. Digital rights are human rights, and it’s important to promote a secure and open internet, free of messages that deceive and undermine trust and hinder the progress made by digital development initiatives.

For programs wanting to learn more to combat the spread of disinformation, the USAID Disinformation Primer is a valuable resource offering a list of recommendations for implementation.
